The Screen Between Us

Last year my best friend stopped coming to school for two weeks. Nobody had hit her or shouted at her. A group chat had done the damage, one cruel message at a time. So when people say technology has done more good than harm for young South Africans, I hesitate, although in the end I agree with them.

The harm is real. Cyberbullying follows a child home in a way that playground bullying never could. Fake news spreads faster than the truth, and many teenagers measure their worth in likes. We should not pretend these wounds are small.

Nevertheless, the good is larger and it reaches further. In a country where many schools have no laboratory, a simulation on a cheap smartphone lets a learner see a chemical reaction. Young entrepreneurs in Soweto market their businesses to thousands without paying for a billboard. During the pandemic, technology was the only classroom that stayed open.

What matters, then, is guidance. A knife can prepare a meal or cause an injury; we teach children to use it rather than banning kitchens. If parents and schools teach digital responsibility, the benefits will far outweigh the costs.

My friend returned to school. She now runs an online page against bullying, using the same tool that hurt her to heal others.
